# Naga

Naga are swift, watchful reptilian people who place great weight on honorable conduct. Their ancestors praised courage, restraint, and keeping one's word, yet denied those same duties to the other intelligent peoples of their homeworld. The Naga of the Sphere inherited both the ideal and its failure. What honor demands now remains a living argument among them.

## Appearance and Biology

Naga have scales that can be any color or combination of colors, long tails, horns on their heads, and catlike slit pupils. Scale color does not establish ancestry, rank, or character. A shift of the head or tail may show attention or discomfort, but it does not reveal what someone intends.

Most Naga are cold-blooded. Warmth helps them move and react at their best, while cold slows them. Early Naga planned work around sunlight and warmed their homes; later generations developed heated everyday clothing that kept them comfortable wherever they worked. Modern garments serve the same purpose on ships and in mixed-species cities. Layered robes, once practical sources of warmth and marks of family identity, are now worn mainly for rituals, festivals, and celebrations. Individuals vary in their tolerance for cold and in how they manage it.

Their characteristic **Instinct** appears in alertness to movement and changes in their surroundings. Their **Agility** appears in swiftness, flexibility, quick turns, and recovery of balance. These strengths can serve a scout or fighter, but also a dancer, climber, rescue worker, or parent catching a child before a fall.

## Culture: The Measure of Honor

### A Deed and Its Consequences

Naga archives call their ancestral tradition of honor *the Measure*. It asks people to stand by what they do, especially when admitting a mistake will cost them. A guard who holds a post through danger may earn respect. So may a guard who leaves that post to rescue trapped civilians and then reports the breach. The Measure asks whom each choice protected, whom it harmed, and whether the guard will answer for the result. Naga have never agreed on every answer.

Most Naga favor a calm manner. A pause before speaking can show care; restraint in a fight can keep anger from choosing the next move. Calmness is a practiced habit, not an inborn virtue, and a quiet voice proves nothing about a person's intentions.

### Pledges, Witnesses, and Repair

A pledge of service once bound a Naga to a household, village, teacher, or cause. The obligation ran both ways: leaders owed protection, provisions, and a fair hearing to those who served them. Abandoning wounded retainers could stain a leader's name even after a victory. Families taught children to remember help they had received, while some also taught them to question elders who used that gratitude to demand obedience.

In a formal dispute, both sides named the harm before witnesses. The person responsible could answer, establish the facts, and offer repair. An apology carried more weight when followed by action: replacing stolen tools, caring for someone injured, or giving up a position won through deceit. A public harm usually called for a public answer. Wealthy houses often found compensation easier than ordinary workers did, leaving Naga society to argue whether status could purchase an honorable reputation.

### Fighting with Honor

Traditional fighting schools taught students to read an opponent's movement, act swiftly, and stop when the need for violence ended. A declared challenge might be expected in a duel but dangerous when protecting someone from an immediate threat. The question was not simply whether a fighter followed a ritual; it was whether they could answer for the harm they chose to cause. Carefully maintained blades remain ceremonial objects as well as weapons. A dockworker who returns a lost ration allotment may show the Measure more clearly than a decorated officer who ignores a hungry crew.

### The Boundary of Honor

The ancestral Measure had a terrible limit. Many Naga applied its duties only to other Naga. A house could care for a rival's wounded while denying that the Veyl or Orun of Seyra deserved the same protection. Some Naga objected, but their objections did not prevent conquest and eradication. Modern Naga cannot tell the story of their honor without also confronting whom their ancestors excluded from it.

## History of Seyra

### Stone Age — Three Peoples

The ancestral world **Seyra** had warm river valleys, colder uplands, and inhabited coasts. Three intelligent species developed societies there: the Naga, the broad-bodied **Veyl** of the high country, and the water-dwelling **Orun** of the coasts and river mouths. Each made tools, raised children, and passed down knowledge. Early Naga communities tended fires, sheltered their own travelers from the cold, and told stories about keeping watch. That hospitality seldom reached the other peoples.

### Agricultural Age — Closed Valleys

Farming and irrigation supported permanent Naga villages. They guarded their valleys, traded mainly with one another, and treated encounters with Veyl and Orun as threats to land and water. Leaders taught children to distrust both peoples. Accounts of raids preserved Naga losses but rarely recorded what the other side had suffered. Agreements across the boundary were temporary conveniences, not pledges of honor. This isolation and prejudice were taught and enforced; they were not features of Naga biology.

### Age of Cities and Metals — The Houses at War

Powerful houses organized towns, trained fighters, and recorded obligations. Rival Naga houses fought under elaborate rules while waging campaigns against Veyl and Orun communities with few restraints. Wars over upland routes targeted the Veyl; wars over rivers and coastlines targeted the Orun. Naga forces destroyed settlements, seized territory, and kept survivors from rebuilding. Victory chronicles gave the conquered few names. Other surviving records preserve Naga witnesses who condemned the campaigns, though they could not stop them.

### Industrial Age — One People Left

Industry extended the reach of Naga transport, weapons, and surveillance. Coordinated campaigns completed centuries of conquest: the Veyl and Orun were eradicated, leaving Naga as Seyra's only surviving intelligent people. Official monuments called this the unification of the world. Private accounts described empty towns and broken coastal habitats. Some houses celebrated; others preserved the names and records of those they had destroyed. No apology could restore them.

Factories and dense cities also changed Naga life. Workers, engineers, and physicians argued that service to the living mattered more than inherited rank. Public investigations began to replace private settlements after preventable deaths. Reliable heated clothing freed most Naga from depending on a warm room or the day's sun, while the older layered garments became ceremonial dress.

### Electronic Age — Two Paths Forward

Widespread records and communication made it harder to tell Seyra's wars solely through victory monuments. Historians gathered maps, testimony, and accounts of the Veyl and Orun. Traditionalists defended the houses, rituals, and martial service that had shaped Naga civilization. Some defended the conquests or refused to discuss them; others preserved the old forms because they believed wrongdoing must remain visible.

Reformers wanted the Measure to apply to every thinking person. They opened archives, revised schools, and sought institutions that could hold even honored leaders accountable. Some rejected inherited titles; others kept ceremonies while changing whom they served. The divide concerned whether loyalty to the past meant defending it or answering for it. Neither side held a single view, and both included people who acted with principle and people who used honor for advantage.

### Space Age — The Last Watch

Naga reached orbit and settled beyond Seyra while that divide remained unresolved. The seed ark's curated records say Seyra's star began to fail and its cities could not be saved. Communities sent biological samples, histories, and art to an automated vessel meant to carry future children to another world. They called the effort the **Last Watch**. Traditionalists insisted on preserving house records and rituals; reformers insisted that the Veyl, the Orun, and the history of their destruction travel too. The ark's children inherited both arguments without meeting their authors.

## Naga in the Sphere

### Landfall and the Old Divide

Some Naga of Cohort Zero slipped out of their ark before Graduation Day and befriended members of the other lineages in Crossroads Valley. Others stayed inside, studied the archives, and later founded communities dedicated to ancestral forms. The old divide took on new urgency. Traditionalists wanted Naga households and rites to endure; those looking forward argued that honor required lasting bonds with other peoples. Seyra's history makes exclusion a charged choice, though neither position belongs to every member of a camp.

### Life in 518 AA

Most Naga now live in mixed societies across the Sphere. A household may keep a witness book of promises and repairs, wear layered dress for festivals, or observe neither custom. Heated clothes let Naga share ships and workplaces with other lineages. Some serve in the Flotilla or among the Corsairs; many have no connection to arms. A Naga Corsair might calmly challenge a superior who hid a failed rescue, refuse an unnecessary duel, or take on a dangerous task to make good on a mistake. The Measure offers a language for judging such acts, but each Naga chooses how to live with it.

## GM Only: The Ancestral Inheritance

The ancestral Naga were absorbed by the Singularity. The Shepherd later restored their lineage aboard a seed ark containing genuine cultural records and a manufactured account of Seyra's stellar destruction. The Last Watch is the story their descendants inherited, not proof of how their ancestors died. The Veyl and Orun, their eradication, the houses, and the debate over the Measure survive in those archives. Modern Naga traditions were reconstructed on Landfall; no house can claim an unbroken institution stretching from Seyra to the Sphere.

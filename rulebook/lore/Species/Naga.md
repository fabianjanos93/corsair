# Naga

Naga are swift, watchful people whose ancestral culture asks a difficult question: what do you do after you have given your word? Their old courts praised a fighter who could win, but honored one who knew when to lower a weapon, protect a defeated rival, or admit a costly mistake. That idea survived the loss of their homeworld and still shapes Naga families, crews, and disputes across the Sphere.

## The Crested People

Naga are reptilian humanoids with scaled skin, long tails, and crests of horn and flexible spines. Their scales may be any color or combination of colors. A pale, dark, bright, or patterned hide says nothing reliable about ancestry, rank, or temperament. Crests and tails can signal attention, discomfort, or excitement, though their movements are no surer guide to honesty than a human expression.

Most Naga are cold-blooded. Warmth helps them move and react at their best; cold slows them and calls for shelter, insulation, or heated clothing. On their ancestral world, a family might plan work around the morning sun. In the Sphere, heated rooms and tailored garments let Naga work in habitats and ships built for many lineages. A chilled Naga is not helpless, and a warm one is not automatically dangerous. Naga vary in their tolerance and in how they manage it.

Their characteristic **Instinct** reflects an alertness to movement and changes in their surroundings. Their **Agility** appears in quick turns, sure footing, and precise movements. These are common strengths, not a duty to become a scout or warrior. A Naga may put the same gifts to use as a surgeon, dancer, mechanic, or parent catching a child before a fall.

## Ancestral Culture: The Weight of a Deed

Naga archives remember *the Measure*, a broad tradition of judging honor through conduct rather than birth or appearance. To act honorably was to accept responsibility for what one had chosen, especially when the consequences were inconvenient. A guard who stayed at a post through danger earned respect; so did the guard who left it to save trapped civilians and then reported the breach openly. Neither action was beyond argument. The Measure gave people a way to ask whom an act had protected, whom it had harmed, and whether its author would answer for it.

A Naga could pledge service to a household, a village, a teacher, or a cause. Such pledges carried obligations in both directions. Leaders owed food, protection, and a fair hearing to those who served them. A leader who sent retainers into a fight and abandoned their wounded could lose standing even after a victory. Families taught children to remember who had helped them, but also to challenge elders who used gratitude as a claim to obedience. This made honor a living argument, not a list of fixed gestures.

In formal disputes, each side named its grievance before witnesses. The accused could answer, make restitution, or request time to establish the facts. An apology mattered most when paired with repair: returning stolen tools, providing care after an injury, or surrendering a position gained through deception. Private reconciliation was possible, but a public harm usually called for a public answer. Wealthy houses often found it easier to offer compensation than ordinary workers did, and the question of whether status could purchase honor troubled Naga society for centuries.

Combat became one visible test of the Measure, but never its whole meaning. Traditional fighting schools trained students to read an opponent's movement, act decisively, and stop when the need for violence ended. Some taught a bow or a declared challenge before a contest; others held that a warning could endanger the people one was sworn to protect. Striking an unarmed enemy could be a disgrace in a duel and a necessity when that enemy reached for a detonator. Naga stories return to such choices because a clean rule is easiest to keep when little is at stake.

Layered robes, fitted travel clothes, and carefully maintained blades appear throughout the surviving art. A formal garment could bear a family's colors, a record of service, or a patch marking an error its wearer had made good. None was required to live honorably. A dockworker who returned a lost ration allotment might earn more regard than a decorated officer who looked away from a crew's hunger.

## History: From Warm Valleys to the Last Watch

### Early Settlements — The Shared Hearth

The Naga ancestral world, **Seyra**, held warm river valleys divided by colder uplands. Early communities gathered around reliable sources of heat, water, and food. Seasonal cold could strand a traveler or ruin a harvest, so neighbors kept watch paths open and offered shelter to people caught far from home. These practical duties became the earliest stories of the Measure: a promise to keep a fire tended meant little unless someone stayed awake to tend it.

### The Houses — Service and Rivalry

As towns grew, households organized irrigation, trade, and defense. Powerful houses took names and colors, trained retainers, and kept written accounts of service and obligation. Their rivalries produced celebrated defenders and brutal wars. Formal challenges sometimes limited bloodshed between armed rivals, yet rules written by the powerful could also keep laborers and outsiders from being heard. Traveling witnesses and local assemblies arose to record grievances beyond any one house's control. Their accounts were imperfect, but they gave a person wronged by a lord a place to speak.

### The Age of Engines — A Broader Measure

Industry moved Naga into dense cities where no household could provide everything its members needed. Workers, engineers, physicians, and transport crews argued that honor belonged to anyone who kept others alive and answered for mistakes. Public investigations replaced some private settlements. A factory owner could no longer bury a fatal accident with a gift to a bereaved family; a crew could demand a record of the failed equipment and the decisions behind it. Old houses adapted, resisted, or lost influence. The Measure endured because people kept disputing who was allowed to claim it.

### The Last Watch — The Ark Archives

The Naga seed ark's curated records say that Seyra's star began to fail and its cities could not be saved. Communities sent biological samples, histories, arguments, and art to an automated vessel meant to carry their descendants to another world. The records call the final effort the **Last Watch**. They praise those who maintained the launch systems, those who insisted on preserving the names of ordinary people, and those who stayed with neighbors who could not leave. The ark's children inherited these accounts without ever meeting their authors.

## Naga in the Sphere

The first Naga on Landfall learned quickly that ancestral pledges had to make room for other people. Some of Cohort Zero slipped out of the ark and made friends across the valley before Graduation Day. Others remained inside, studied the archives, and later built communities meant to preserve the old forms. Both were responding to the same inheritance. The former asked what honor required of the people standing beside them; the latter feared that forgetting the old duties would leave the dead without descendants willing to remember them.

In 518 AA, most Naga live within the Sphere's mixed societies. A household may keep a witness book of promises and repairs, observe a quiet morning warming ritual, or do neither. Some serve in the Flotilla or among the Corsairs; many have no connection to arms. A Naga Corsair might challenge a superior who hides a failed rescue, refuse an unnecessary duel, or accept a dangerous assignment to make good on an earlier mistake. None of these acts belongs to Naga alone. The Measure offers a language for judging them, and every Naga decides how much of that language to keep.

## GM Only: The Ancestral Inheritance

The ancestral Naga were absorbed by the Singularity. The Shepherd later restored their lineage aboard a seed ark with genuine cultural records and a manufactured account of Seyra's destruction. The Last Watch is the story the descendants inherited, not proof of how their ancestors died. The houses, their disputes, and the long argument over the Measure survive as archived history and reconstructed practice; no modern house can claim an unbroken institution stretching from Seyra to Landfall.

# Naga

Naga are swift, watchful reptilian people who place great weight on honorable conduct. Their ancestors praised courage, restraint, and keeping one's word, yet denied those same duties to the other intelligent peoples of their homeworld. The Naga of the Sphere inherited both the ideal and its failure. What honor demands now remains a living argument among them.

## Appearance and Biology

Naga have scales that can be any color or combination of colors, long tails, horns on their heads, and catlike slit pupils. Scale color does not establish ancestry, rank, or character. A shift of the head or tail may show attention or discomfort, but it does not reveal what someone intends.

Naga lay eggs. Families keep them warm and protected until they hatch, then care for the young as they grow. On Seyra, a household might tend its eggs in a shared heated room; modern families use insulated incubators at home or in medical facilities. These methods provide warmth and protection, not a substitute for parents or other caregivers.

Most Naga are cold-blooded. Warmth helps them move and react at their best, while cold slows them. Early Naga planned work around sunlight and warmed their homes; later generations developed heated everyday clothing that kept them comfortable wherever they worked. Modern garments serve the same purpose on ships and in mixed-species cities. Layered robes, once practical sources of warmth and marks of family identity, are now worn mainly for rituals, festivals, and celebrations. Individuals vary in their tolerance for cold and in how they manage it.

Their characteristic **Instinct** appears in alertness to movement and changes in their surroundings. Their **Agility** appears in swiftness, flexibility, quick turns, and recovery of balance. These strengths can serve a scout or fighter, but also a dancer, climber, rescue worker, or parent catching a child before a fall.

## Culture: The Measure of Honor

### A Deed and Its Consequences

Naga archives call their ancestral tradition of honor *the Measure*. It asks people to stand by what they do, especially when admitting a mistake will cost them. A guard who holds a post through danger may earn respect. So may a guard who leaves that post to rescue trapped civilians and then reports the breach. The Measure asks whom each choice protected, whom it harmed, and whether the guard will answer for the result. Naga have never agreed on every answer.

Most Naga favor a calm manner. A pause before speaking can show care; restraint in a fight can keep anger from choosing the next move. Calmness is a practiced habit, not an inborn virtue, and a quiet voice proves nothing about a person's intentions.

### Pledges, Witnesses, and Repair

A pledge of service once bound a Naga to a household, village, teacher, or cause. The obligation ran both ways: leaders owed protection, provisions, and a fair hearing to those who served them. Abandoning wounded retainers could stain a leader's name even after a victory. Families taught children to remember help they had received, while some also taught them to question elders who used that gratitude to demand obedience.

In a formal dispute, both sides named the harm before witnesses. The person responsible could answer, establish the facts, and offer repair. An apology carried more weight when followed by action: replacing stolen tools, caring for someone injured, or giving up a position won through deceit. A public harm usually called for a public answer. Wealthy houses often found compensation easier than ordinary workers did, leaving Naga society to argue whether status could purchase an honorable reputation.

### Fighting with Honor

Traditional fighting schools taught students to read an opponent's movement, act swiftly, and stop when the need for violence ended. A declared challenge might be expected in a duel but dangerous when protecting someone from an immediate threat. The question was not simply whether a fighter followed a ritual; it was whether they could answer for the harm they chose to cause. Carefully maintained blades remain ceremonial objects as well as weapons. A dockworker who returns a lost ration allotment may show the Measure more clearly than a decorated officer who ignores a hungry crew.

### The Boundary of Honor

The ancestral Measure had a terrible limit. Many Naga applied its duties only to other Naga. A house could care for a rival's wounded while denying that the Veyl or Orun of Seyra deserved the same protection. This exclusion helped sustain a long three-way war and, eventually, the Naga victory that eradicated both peoples. Modern Naga cannot tell the story of their honor without also confronting whom their ancestors excluded from it.

## Belief: The Spirits of Seyra

An old Naga belief holds that every natural phenomenon, place, and thing has a spirit or lesser god connected to it. A river has one, but so do a bend in its course, a stone beside it, and the storm that floods it. These spirits are not necessarily kind or powerful. A household may offer thanks to the warmth of its hearth; travelers may acknowledge the wind before a crossing. Such gestures express respect for the world and for the consequences of using it.

There is no single temple or priesthood that speaks for every spirit. Families, neighborhoods, and regions keep different observances. Some Naga pray, some use the old rites as ways to remember a place or a person, and others do not believe in spirits at all. A heated garment can be blessed before its first use without anyone claiming that its warmth comes from the blessing. Even among believers, the faith has no settled answer to the war: some ancestral houses invoked spirits to defend conquest, while later thinkers asked what it meant to honor a river after destroying the people who lived beside it.

## History of Seyra

### Stone Age — Three Peoples

The ancestral world **Seyra** had one immense landmass, several smaller islands, warm river valleys, colder uplands, and inhabited coasts. Three intelligent species developed societies there: the Naga, the broad-bodied **Veyl** of the high country, and the water-dwelling **Orun** of the coasts and river mouths. Each made tools, raised children, and passed down knowledge. The three peoples competed over routes, water, and territory from their earliest recorded encounters. Naga communities tended fires and sheltered their own travelers from the cold, but seldom extended that care to Veyl or Orun. Raids and reprisals made the early centuries bloody, and isolation kept each people from learning much about the others beyond their enemies' actions.

### Agricultural Age — Closed Valleys

Farming and irrigation supported permanent Naga villages. They guarded their valleys, traded mainly with one another, and fought both Veyl and Orun for land and water. The Veyl and Orun fought each other as well: this was a three-way struggle, not a single Naga campaign against a united enemy. Naga leaders taught children to distrust both peoples. Accounts of battles preserved Naga losses but rarely recorded what the other sides had suffered. Agreements across the boundary were temporary conveniences, not pledges of honor. This isolation and prejudice were taught and enforced; they were not features of Naga biology.

### Age of Cities and Metals — The Houses at War

Powerful houses organized towns, trained fighters, and recorded obligations. Rival Naga houses fought under elaborate rules but joined forces against Veyl and Orun armies whenever it served them. The war spread across the great landmass and its islands. Each people won ground and lost it again; the Veyl held the uplands, the Orun contested waterways and coasts, and Naga houses fought to control the routes between them. Naga leaders increasingly taught that victory justified any means used against another species. They destroyed settlements, seized food and territory, and prevented survivors from rebuilding. Their victory chronicles gave the conquered few names.

### Industrial Age — One People Left

Naga industry advanced faster than that of their rivals. Rail transport, mass production, weapons, and surveillance let Naga forces supply and coordinate campaigns across Seyra's huge landmass. Their leaders paired that advantage with a ruthless doctrine: any cost was acceptable if it secured final victory. After generations of three-way war, they eradicated the Veyl and Orun, leaving Naga as Seyra's only surviving intelligent people. Across Naga society, the result was celebrated as a complete victory and the unification of the world. Monuments honored its commanders, and public histories praised the methods that had won. The destruction was not widely treated as a national mistake in this age.

Factories and dense cities also changed Naga life. Workers, engineers, and physicians argued that service to the living mattered more than inherited rank. Public investigations began to replace private settlements after preventable deaths. Reliable heated clothing freed most Naga from depending on a warm room or the day's sun, while the older layered garments became ceremonial dress.

### Electronic Age — Two Paths Forward

Widespread records and communication made it harder to tell Seyra's wars solely through victory monuments. Historians gathered maps, battlefield orders, testimony, and the remaining records of the Veyl and Orun. Later Naga philosophers came to agree that their extinction had been a mistake. They did not agree on what course their ancestors should have taken instead: negotiated boundaries, shared rule, withdrawal, and other proposals all had advocates. Speciesism became a sensitive subject because the old belief that only Naga deserved the Measure had helped make the war possible.

Traditionalists defended the houses, rituals, and martial service that had shaped Naga civilization. Some resisted the new account of the war; others accepted the philosophers' judgment while insisting that the old forms still held value. Reformers wanted the Measure to apply to every thinking person. They opened archives, revised schools, and sought institutions that could hold even honored leaders accountable. The divide concerned how to carry the past forward, not whether the Veyl and Orun could be brought back. Neither side held a single view.

### Space Age — The Last Watch

Naga reached orbit and settled beyond Seyra while that divide remained unresolved. The seed ark's curated records say Seyra's star began to fail and its cities could not be saved. Communities sent biological samples, histories, and art to an automated vessel meant to carry future children to another world. They called the effort the **Last Watch**. Traditionalists insisted on preserving house records, rites, and accounts of the spirits; reformers insisted that the Veyl, the Orun, and the history of their destruction travel too. The ark's children inherited both arguments without meeting their authors.

## Naga in the Sphere

### Landfall and the Old Divide

Some Naga of Cohort Zero slipped out of their ark before Graduation Day and befriended members of the other lineages in Crossroads Valley. Others stayed inside, studied the archives, and later founded communities dedicated to ancestral forms. The old divide took on new urgency. Traditionalists wanted Naga households and rites to endure; those looking forward argued that honor required lasting bonds with other peoples. Seyra's history makes exclusion a charged choice, though neither position belongs to every member of a camp.

### Life in 518 AA

Most Naga now live in mixed societies across the Sphere. A household may keep a witness book of promises and repairs, wear layered dress for festivals, thank the spirit of a new home, or observe none of these customs. Families warm and protect their eggs in homes and clinics. Heated clothes let Naga share ships and workplaces with other lineages. Some serve in the Flotilla or among the Corsairs; many have no connection to arms. A Naga Corsair might calmly challenge a superior who hid a failed rescue, refuse an unnecessary duel, or take on a dangerous task to make good on a mistake. The Measure offers a language for judging such acts, but each Naga chooses how to live with it.

## Radical Groups: Origins and Modern Forms

The ark preserved ancestral houses, arguments over the war, and beliefs about Seyra's spirits. After Landfall, some Naga rebuilt these traditions into movements with sharper demands than most Naga share. These three groups draw on different parts of the inheritance; none represents all traditionalists, reformers, or believers.

### The Unbroken Banner — Victory Without Apology

**Origins — The Victorious Houses.** Industrial-age houses celebrated the defeat of the Veyl and Orun as Seyra's greatest achievement. Their monuments taught that survival proved the rightness of their methods. The Unbroken Banner take this account at face value and reject the later philosophers' judgment.

**Nowadays — Species Supremacists.** The Unbroken Banner recruit from Naga who resent mixed settlements and fear losing their ancestral identity. They stage martial ceremonies, circulate selective war histories, and pressure Naga families to cut ties with other lineages. Their militant cells intimidate mixed households and target institutions that teach the full history of Seyra. Other Naga traditionalists often oppose them, holding that a rite loses its honor when used to excuse cruelty.

### The Open Measure — Honor for Every Person

**Origins — The Electronic-Age Reformers.** Philosophers and archivists who studied the extinction of the Veyl and Orun argued that the Measure failed because it excluded other thinking beings. Reformers opened records and sought public accountability, though they disagreed on what their ancestors should have done instead. The Open Measure turn that dispute into a demand for action now.

**Nowadays — Militant Reckoners.** Members expose concealed abuses, recover suppressed archives, and organize protection for people targeted by speciesist groups. Their harder factions leak private records without consent, occupy memorials, or attack the property of families they hold responsible for inherited honors. They argue that polite debate has protected the old hierarchy for too long. Many Naga who agree with their diagnosis reject these methods, especially when they harm people who had no part in the wrong.

### The Hearthwardens — Keep Faith with the Spirits

**Origins — The Local Shrines.** On Seyra, families acknowledged spirits connected to rivers, hearths, storms, and ordinary objects. Some caretakers held that a place deserved respect even when no person could speak for it. The Hearthwardens draw on that duty and on the later question of how Naga could honor Seyra while destroying lives and habitats upon it.

**Nowadays — Sacred-Site Militants.** Hearthwardens tend small shrines, restore damaged places, and challenge extraction or construction that threatens sites they consider sacred. Their militant circles block access, disable equipment, or remove artifacts from museums and private collections to return them to a place's care. They disagree among themselves about whether a spirit can be honored aboard a ship or in a new settlement. Most Naga who observe the old rites do so peacefully and do not belong to the group.

## GM Only: The Ancestral Inheritance

The ancestral Naga were absorbed by the Singularity. The Shepherd later restored their lineage aboard a seed ark containing genuine cultural records and a manufactured account of Seyra's stellar destruction. The Last Watch is the story their descendants inherited, not proof of how their ancestors died. The Veyl and Orun, their eradication, the houses, and the debate over the Measure survive in those archives. Modern Naga traditions were reconstructed on Landfall; no house can claim an unbroken institution stretching from Seyra to the Sphere.
